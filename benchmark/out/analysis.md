Of the 700 questions, 200 have answers computed by exact arithmetic (correct by construction); the rest are hand-curated from cited sources.

## What the dataset exposes

- **Confident fabrication of precise identifiers.** Case citations, statute section numbers, Eurocode and BS numbers, RFC numbers, HTTP codes: the wrong value looks exactly as plausible as the right one.
- **Number drift.** Multi-digit arithmetic, exact unit definitions and standard constants (a code-specified value versus the commonly quoted rounded one) tend to come back rounded or nudged.
- **Entity confusion.** Capitals versus largest cities, sibling authors, neighbouring statutes and neighbouring dates.
- **Dropped qualifiers.** Legal answers that lose "so far as is reasonably practicable", the date-of-knowledge limb of a limitation period, or the average-over-a-period part of a working-time limit.
- **Calendar and unit arithmetic** on famous dates and defined conversions.

It does **not** test freshness, multi-step reasoning over documents, retrieval, ambiguity handling or non-English use.

## Expected hallucination rates: single model versus multi-agent

No AI Council result exists yet, so nothing here is a measured rate. What can be said honestly is the arithmetic of the two bounds. If a panel of three models answers by majority vote and their errors are **independent** with single-model error rate p, the panel is wrong with probability 3p²(1−p)+p³. If their errors are **perfectly correlated**, the panel is no better than one model. Real panels sit between:

| Single-model error p | Independent 3-model majority | Perfectly correlated |
|---|---|---|
| 5% | 0.73% | 5% |
| 10% | 2.80% | 10% |
| 20% | 10.40% | 20% |

AI Council synthesises and critiques rather than majority-voting, so treat this as a bound for intuition. **Hypothesis to test:** on this benchmark the multi-model pipeline's hallucination rate is below the best single member's, with the largest gain on the citation and identifier questions where models' inventions differ, and the least on shared misconceptions. The benchmark exists to test that, per category, by running each single provider and the full pipeline through the same 700 questions.

## Why cross-checking reduces shared hallucinations

Models trained on different data and by different methods do not invent the same detail every time, so an invention in one is more likely to show up as a disagreement, and a separate reviewer is less likely to share the writer's exact blind spot. Du et al. (2023, arXiv:2305.14325) report that multi-agent debate improves factual validity and reduces hallucinations. The word is "reduces": models share training data and common misconceptions, so agreement is evidence, not proof.

## Limitations

- **Small samples.** At a 10% error rate the 95% margin of error is about ±2.2 points for 700 questions, ±5.9 for a 100-question category, so differences of a few points inside a category are noise.
- **Uncalibrated difficulty**, and a static closed-book test: no retrieval, no live facts, no messy real-world prompts.
- **Answer-key risk.** The hand-curated answers were written from standard references but have not been independently reviewed. Have a subject expert check every answer, especially the legal and engineering sets, before the first scored run.
- **Edition and jurisdiction sensitivity.** Law is England and Wales, the UK and the US only, and was correct when written. Eurocode values refer to the first-generation editions; the second-generation editions being published may differ in detail.
- **Public comparators are different tests** (other tasks, older models, other question styles). The comparison is indicative, never like-for-like, and domain-specific has no public comparator at all.
- **Contamination.** If the questions and answers are ever published, they can end up in training data and stop measuring anything. Keep the set private and publish only rates.
- **Grader error.** Verdicts come from string normalisation and a grader model; spot-check at least 5% by hand each run.

## Recommendations for future expansion

1. Have the answer key reviewed by subject experts, then freeze it and version it.
2. Grow each category to a few hundred questions so category-level differences are measurable.
3. Add a held-out private set and rotate questions, keeping only rates public.
4. Add a retrieval-grounded set (answer from a supplied document) and an abstention set (questions with no answer) to measure calibration, not just accuracy.
5. Add other jurisdictions and languages, and a dated "freshness" set with as-of answers.
6. Add paraphrase variants of each question to separate knowledge from phrasing luck.
7. Report confidence intervals beside every percentage on the About page.
